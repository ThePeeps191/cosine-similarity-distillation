\begin{abstract}
	Traditional knowledge distillation (KD) requires inference of the teacher model at every batch during student training in order to transfer knowledge, causing extensive GPU memory costs and compromising the teacher's proprietary weights and architecture by exposing them. We propose Cosine Similarity Distillation (CSD), a distillation method that precomputes the cosine similarity between the teacher's intermediate features and a frozen random reference matrix to produce compact \emph{fingerprints} for each training sample. This precomputation phase only happens once before student training, and after fingerprints have been generated, the teacher can be discarded to be completely removed from the training loop. The student is then trained to match these fingerprints via regression alongside its classification loss. When tested on the CIFAR-100 dataset with ResNet-20/56 student and teacher architectures, CSD retains 49\% of the accuracy gain from standard KD while reducing the stored teacher-side information from the full 3.29\,MB model to per-class averaged fingerprints as small as 50\,KB (a 67$\times$ reduction). Our results establish CSD as a practical, privacy-friendly point on the accuracy-storage-privacy Pareto frontier for distillation.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{knowledge distillation \and cosine similarity \and random projections \and teacher-free \and efficient ML}

\section{Introduction}

Knowledge Distillation (KD)~\citep{hinton2015distilling} is a widely used model compression technique in which a smaller but more efficient student model is trained to mimic the behavior of a larger, more accurate teacher to allow for the student to achieve better accuracy compared to training from scratch because it is able to receive guidance from the pre-trained teacher model. This is usually done by matching either the teacher's softened output distributions via KL divergence or its intermediate representations~\citep{romero2015fitnets}. However, all such methods require the teacher to be loaded and forwarded at every step during student training, which incurs three fundamental costs that limit deployment in resource-constrained or privacy-sensitive settings where the full teacher cannot be shared or kept in memory:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Memory cost:} Both models must reside in GPU memory simultaneously during distillation, limiting student size and batch throughput.
    \item \textbf{Storage cost:} The full teacher must be stored and distributed alongside the student training pipeline.
	\item \textbf{Privacy cost:} The teacher's weights and architecture are exposed, which creates a liability when training on sensitive data or deploying to untrusted environments.
\end{enumerate}

We propose \emph{Cosine Similarity Distillation (CSD)}, a distillation method that eliminates the teacher from the student training loop entirely. Before training, we forward the training set through the teacher in a one-time precomputation phase, extract features at a chosen intermediate layer, and compute their cosine similarities with a frozen random matrix~$\mathbf{R}$. The resulting compact vector, referred to as the \emph{fingerprint}, encodes the directional structure of the teacher's feature space. The teacher is then discarded. During student training, the student's features at the same layer are projected onto the same random reference matrix, and the model is trained to match the precomputed fingerprints alongside the cross-entropy loss. The matrix~$\mathbf{R}$ functions as a shared coordinate system that makes the comparison well-defined.

This approach offers three key advantages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Teacher-free training:} All teacher computation occurs in a one-time offline precomputation phase, so during the computationally intensive student training phase (200 epochs), only the student resides in GPU memory. By contrast, KD and FitNet each forward the teacher 78,200 times during student training (once per batch).
    \item \textbf{Extreme storage efficiency}: The information carried forward from the teacher is dramatically compressed. Per-class averaged fingerprints encode the full CIFAR-100 dataset in 50\,KB (67$\times$ smaller than the 3.29\,MB teacher model).
	\item \textbf{Deployment decoupling:} Because fingerprints are precomputed, the teacher never needs to be shared or accessed during student training. This means the teacher can be run in a secure environment to produce fingerprints, and only the lightweight fingerprint table, and not the full model, needs to be distributed for downstream training. Additionally, since fingerprints are derived from pooled intermediate activations rather than raw inputs, reconstructing training images from fingerprints is substantially harder than from raw data.
\end{enumerate}

We evaluate CSD on CIFAR-100 with ResNet-56 as teacher and ResNet-20 as student. CSD recovers 48.9\% of KD's accuracy gain over the student-only baseline while achieving the storage, memory, and privacy advantages above. We present ablation studies on fingerprint dimension r and loss weight $\lambda$, and we validate the core mechanism by measuring the cosine similarity between student and teacher fingerprints during training.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Knowledge Distillation}

Knowledge distillation was formalized by Hinton et al.~\citep{hinton2015distilling}, who train a student to match the softened output distribution of a teacher. The KD loss is:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = (1-\alpha) \cdot \text{CE}(S(x), y) + \alpha \cdot \tau^2 \cdot \text{KL}\left(\sigma(T(x)/\tau) \;\|\; \sigma(S(x)/\tau)\right)
\label{eq:kd}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ is the softmax function, $\tau$ is the temperature, and $\alpha$ balances the two terms. The teacher must be forwarded at every batch to produce $T(x)$, resulting in $N_{\text{batches}} \times N_{\text{epochs}}$ teacher forwards. For our setting (128 batch size, 200 epochs), this amounts to 78,200 forwards.

FitNet~\citep{romero2015fitnets} extends KD to the feature level by minimizing the mean-squared error between intermediate representations:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FitNet}} = \text{CE}(S(x), y) + \beta \cdot \text{MSE}(f_S(x), f_T(x))
\label{eq:fitnet}
\end{equation}

where $f_S$ and $f_T$ are pooled features from a chosen layer. FitNet requires teacher forwards at every batch (78,200 per training run; the 234,600 total in our experiments includes a grid search over $\beta \in \{10, 50, 100\}$, analogous to the hyperparameter tuning performed for CSD). Attention Transfer~\citep{zagoruyko2017paying} follows the same pattern.

\subsection{Cosine Similarity in Distillation}

CosPress~\citep{mannix2024preserving} preserves cosine similarities between image embeddings in the feature space, but it still requires the teacher model at training time and uses learned projection layers. CSD differs fundamentally in two ways: (a) the projection matrix $\mathbf{R}$ is random and frozen (no learning is involved) and (b) the teacher is completely discarded after fingerprint precomputation.

\subsection{Teacher-Free Distillation}

Tailored Coordinate System (TCS)~\citep{zhou2024tcs} uses Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the teacher's feature activations to construct a coordinate system for teacher-free distillation. While TCS shares our goal of eliminating the teacher from training, it requires a data-dependent PCA computation on the full training set, which itself requires loading the teacher. CSD uses random projections, which are data-independent, simpler to compute, and provide a tunable privacy-accuracy trade-off via the fingerprint dimension $r$.

\subsection{Random Projections in Machine Learning}

The Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma guarantees that random projections approximately preserve pairwise distances in high-dimensional spaces~\citep{johnson1984extensions, ghojogh2021johnson}. Random projections have been used extensively for dimensionality reduction, sketching, and compressed sensing. To the best of our knowledge, CSD is the first method to use random projections as a shared coordinate system for knowledge distillation---precomputing teacher feature directions in this common space of random directions and training a student to match them.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Notation and Preliminaries}

Let $T$ denote the teacher model and $S$ denote the student model. Both are CIFAR variants of ResNet~\citep{he2016deep}, with three residual stages producing feature maps of increasing channel depth. We extract features after the third residual stage, before global average pooling. For an input image $x$, the pooled feature vector at this layer is

\begin{equation}
f(x) = \text{AvgPool}(\text{layer3}(x)) \in \mathbb{R}^{C}
\end{equation}

where $C = 64$ is the channel count. The teacher (ResNet-56) and student (ResNet-20) differ in depth but share the same channel dimensions at all layers, enabling direct fingerprint comparison.

\subsection{Random Reference Matrix}

We construct a frozen random reference matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times r}$ where $r = 128$ (default fingerprint dimension). Each column is drawn independently from a standard Gaussian distribution and then L2-normalized to unit length:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{:,j} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_C), \qquad
\mathbf{R}_{:,j} = \frac{\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{:,j}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{:,j}\|_2} \quad \text{so that} \quad \|\mathbf{R}_{:,j}\|_2 = 1 \;\; (j = 1, \dots, r).
\end{equation}

The matrix $\mathbf{R}$ is frozen---it receives no gradients and is never updated. Its storage cost is $64 \times 128 \times 4 = 32$\,KB.

For multi-layer fingerprints, we also construct $\mathbf{R}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{32 \times r}$ for layer-2 features (32 channels, $16\times16$ spatial resolution), L2-normalized identically. To avoid ambiguity, we hereafter refer to the layer-3 matrix as $\mathbf{R}_3$ (previously $\mathbf{R}$).

We note that for the layer-3 case ($C = 64$), the default fingerprint dimension $r = 128$ means the random matrix performs an expansion rather than compression at the feature-vector level. The storage reduction in per-class CSD comes from averaging 50,000 fingerprints into 100 class centroids, not from the random projection itself. The matrix $\mathbf{R}_3$'s role is to provide a shared coordinate system for alignment, not dimensionality reduction.

\subsection{Teacher Fingerprint Generation}

Before student training begins, we perform a one-time forward pass over the training set through the teacher. To ensure the student can match these fingerprints under augmentation (RandomCrop, RandomHorizontalFlip), we compute fingerprints from the same augmented views the student sees and average them. Concretely, for each of the 50,000 training images we generate $N_a = 8$ augmented views, yielding 400,000 teacher forward passes in total---all completed before student training starts.

For each image $x_i$ with label $y_i$:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Generate $N_a$ augmented views $x_i^{(1)}, \dots, x_i^{(N_a)}$ using the student's training augmentations (default $N_a = 8$).
    \item For each view, pool layer-3 features, L2-normalize, and compute the cosine similarities against the columns of $\mathbf{R}_3$:
    \begin{equation}
    \phi_i^{(k)} = \frac{\text{AvgPool}(\text{layer3}(x_i^{(k)}))}{\|\text{AvgPool}(\text{layer3}(x_i^{(k)}))\|_2} \mathbf{R}_3 \quad \in \mathbb{R}^{r}
    \end{equation}
    \item Average across augmented views: $\phi_i = \frac{1}{N_a} \sum_{k=1}^{N_a} \phi_i^{(k)}$.
\end{enumerate}

Fingerprints are stored in dataset order so that index $i$ corresponds to the $i$-th training sample.

For multi-layer fingerprints, we additionally compute layer-2 fingerprints using $\mathbf{R}_2$ identically.

In addition to per-sample fingerprints $\{\phi_i\}_{i=1}^{50000}$, we compute per-class averaged fingerprints by averaging over all samples in each class:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:class_fp}
\phi^{(c)} = \frac{1}{|\{i : y_i = c\}|} \sum_{i: y_i = c} \phi_i \quad \text{for } c = 1, \dots, 100
\end{equation}

The per-class fingerprint table requires $100 \times 128 \times 4 = 50$\,KB of storage. (Throughout this paper, storage figures use binary units---KiB and MiB---for internal consistency; the teacher's 3.29\,MB corresponds to 3.29\,MiB.) For multi-layer fingerprints the per-class storage doubles to approximately 100\,KB (50\,KB for layer-3 plus 50\,KB for layer-2 at $r=128$), still negligible relative to the teacher model.

\textbf{Per-sample vs.\ per-class.} Both variants share the same underlying fingerprint computation: the same random matrix $\mathbf{R}_3$, the same 50,000 individual fingerprints $\phi_i$, and the same $N_a=8$ augmentation averaging. The only difference is which target the student regresses against during training. In per-sample CSD, the student matches the individual fingerprint $\phi_i$ of each specific image, preserving per-example teacher geometry but requiring 24.41\,MB of stored fingerprints. In per-class CSD, all fingerprints belonging to the same class are averaged into a single centroid $\phi^{(c)}$ via Eq.~\ref{eq:class_fp}, reducing storage to 50\,KB. This averaging can act as a denoising operation: averaging over many samples suppresses instance-level variation in the fingerprints, producing a cleaner regression target. This helps explain why per-class CSD can outperform per-sample CSD (69.65\% vs.\ 69.17\% in our experiments) in this single-seed study, although differing optimal $\lambda$ values may also contribute to the gap.

After fingerprint generation, the teacher model is discarded. Only the fingerprint tables and the random matrices $\mathbf{R}_2$, $\mathbf{R}_3$ are retained.

\subsection{Student Training with CSD}

For each training batch, the student sees randomly augmented images and computes fingerprints at the same layer(s):

\begin{equation}
\phi_S(x_{\text{aug}}) = \frac{\text{AvgPool}(\text{layer3}_S(x_{\text{aug}}))}{\|\text{AvgPool}(\text{layer3}_S(x_{\text{aug}}))\|_2} \mathbf{R}_3
\end{equation}

The CSD training loss contains a cross-entropy term for classification and a fingerprint-regression term for teacher alignment:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:csd_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CSD}} = \text{CE}(S(x_{\text{aug}}), y) \;+\; \lambda \cdot \ell_{\text{fp}}(\phi_S, \phi_{\text{target}})
\end{equation}

where $\phi_{\text{target}}$ is the per-sample fingerprint $\phi_i$ for per-sample CSD, or the per-class fingerprint $\phi^{(y)}$ for per-class CSD.

\paragraph{Fingerprint loss.} We use a cosine-similarity-based loss that focuses on directional agreement:

\begin{equation}
\ell_{\text{pull}}(\phi_S, \phi_T) = 1 - \cos(\phi_S, \phi_T)
\end{equation}

where $\cos(\cdot,\cdot)$ denotes cosine similarity. For multi-layer fingerprints, losses from both layers are combined with layer-specific weights:

\begin{equation}
\ell_{\text{fp}} = w_{L2} \cdot \ell_{\text{pull}}(\phi_S^{L2}, \phi_T^{L2}) + w_{L3} \cdot \ell_{\text{pull}}(\phi_S^{L3}, \phi_T^{L3})
\end{equation}

with $w_{L2} = 0.2$ and $w_{L3} = 1.0$ (defaults). The layer-2 (texture/envelope) signal is downweighted relative to the layer-3 (object/semantic) signal.

\paragraph{Lambda warmup.} We linearly ramp $\lambda$ from 0 to its target value over the first 40 epochs, allowing the student to establish basic classification features before fingerprint alignment begins.

\subsection{Why CSD Works}

Four design choices drive CSD's effectiveness:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cosine similarity captures directional agreement.} By L2-normalizing both features and columns of $\mathbf{R}_3$, fingerprint entries depend only on feature direction, not magnitude. This forces the student to match the teacher's geometry rather than its activation scales.
    \item \textbf{The random reference matrix provides a shared coordinate system.} Without $\mathbf{R}_3$, student and teacher features inhabit different high-dimensional spaces with no natural correspondence (different depths, different parameters). The random matrix projects both onto the same set of $r$ random directions, making comparison well-defined.
    \item \textbf{Augmentation-invariant matching.} Teacher fingerprints are computed from the same augmented views the student sees. The student's fingerprint at training time is statistically comparable to the target fingerprint, allowing the cosine loss to produce a meaningful gradient.
    \item \textbf{Class-level target smoothing.} Per-class fingerprints average roughly 500 individual sample fingerprints into a single class centroid. Under the model $\phi_i = \mu^{(c)} + \varepsilon_i$ where $\varepsilon_i$ is zero-mean within-class variation, class averaging reduces the variance of the sample-specific component by approximately $1/N_c$. This provides a more stable regression target during training, which we observe empirically: per-class CSD (69.65\%) outperforms per-sample CSD (69.17\%) in our experiments. We note that this is an observation rather than a theoretical guarantee, as fingerprints within a class are not strictly i.i.d.\ and the cosine loss is nonlinear in $\phi_T$; alternative explanations such as differing optimal $\lambda$ values or target regularization effects cannot be ruled out from a single-seed study.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset and Models}

We use CIFAR-100~\citep{krizhevsky2009learning}, which contains 50,000 training and 10,000 test images of $32 \times 32$ pixels across 100 classes. Training augmentations: RandomCrop(32, padding=4) and RandomHorizontalFlip. Both training and test images are normalized with channel means $(0.5071, 0.4867, 0.4408)$ and standard deviations $(0.2675, 0.2565, 0.2761)$.

Teacher: ResNet-56~\citep{he2016deep} (9 blocks per stage, 861,620 parameters, 3.29\,MB). Student: ResNet-20 (3 blocks per stage, 278,324 parameters, 1.06\,MB). Both follow the CIFAR ResNet convention: conv1 with kernel size 3, stride 1, no max-pooling. Feature extraction: after the third residual stage, before global average pooling, yielding (64, 8, 8) feature maps.

\subsection{Training Details}

All models are trained with SGD (momentum 0.9, weight decay $5 \times 10^{-4}$) and a MultiStepLR learning rate schedule (initial $0.1$, decays by factor 0.2 at epochs 60, 120, and 160). Batch size: 128. Main experiments: 200 epochs; ablation studies: 200 epochs for $\lambda$ sweep, 100 epochs for $r$ sweep.

All experiments use a single seed (42) with deterministic cuDNN operations. Training performed on a single NVIDIA T4 GPU (16\,GB).

\subsection{Baselines and CSD Variants}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Student-only (no distillation):} Cross-entropy loss only. No teacher involved.
    \item \textbf{KD (Hinton):} Loss as in Eq.~\ref{eq:kd}, with $\alpha = 0.9$ and $\tau = 4$.
    \item \textbf{FitNet:} Loss as in Eq.~\ref{eq:fitnet}, with $\beta \in \{10, 50, 100\}$ (best reported).
    \item \textbf{CSD per-sample:} Loss as in Eq.~\ref{eq:csd_loss}, per-sample fingerprints. $\lambda$ tuned over $\{0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0\}$ at 200 epochs.
    \item \textbf{CSD per-class:} Loss as in Eq.~\ref{eq:csd_loss}, per-class averaged fingerprints with separate $\lambda$ tuning.
\end{enumerate}

KD and FitNet require teacher forwarding at every batch. All CSD variants require exactly one teacher loading for fingerprint precomputation (50k images $\times$ 8 augmented views), and the teacher is never loaded during student training.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Storage and Deployment Analysis}

The primary practical advantage of CSD is its storage footprint and its decoupling of teacher computation from student training. Figure~\ref{fig:storage} compares the storage required for knowledge transfer across methods.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{storage_comparison.png}
\caption{Storage comparison (log scale).}
\label{fig:storage}
\end{figure}

Per-class fingerprints are 67$\times$ smaller than the teacher model (50\,KB vs.\ 3.29\,MB) with a small accuracy penalty relative to per-sample fingerprints. The deployment advantage goes beyond storage: KD must keep the teacher model resident in GPU memory alongside the student for the full 200 training epochs (78,200 forward passes interleaved with student training). CSD confines all teacher computation to a one-time offline phase; the teacher is discarded before student training begins and the student trains with no dependence on the teacher whatsoever.

For additional context, a na\"{\i}ve ``Offline KD'' baseline that precomputes and stores the teacher's soft logits for all 50,000 training samples would require $50{,}000 \times 100 \times 4 \approx 19.5$\,MB of storage---nearly six times larger than the teacher model itself and over $300\times$ larger than our per-class fingerprints. This highlights that CSD's storage efficiency comes not merely from offline precomputation but from the compact fingerprint representation enabled by random projection.

\subsection{Privacy Analysis}

CSD provides a teacher-model hiding property rather than a cryptographic privacy guarantee. The fingerprint $\phi = \text{normalize}(f) \cdot \mathbf{R}$ is a linear projection of the normalized feature vector $f$ onto the columns of $\mathbf{R}$. Since $r = 128 > C = 64$ and a random Gaussian matrix has full row rank with probability one, the normalized feature direction $\hat{f} = f / \|f\|_2$ can be recovered from $\phi$ given knowledge of $\mathbf{R}$ via $\hat{f} = \phi\,\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}$. The magnitude $\|f\|_2$ is lost due to the L2 normalization, but the directional information used by CSD for distillation is recoverable. Therefore, CSD should not be described as providing a one-way or information-theoretically hard barrier against feature recovery.

The practical deployment benefit is that CSD eliminates the need to share or distribute the teacher model during student training: the teacher can be run in a trusted environment to produce fingerprints, and only the fingerprint table need be transferred. Furthermore, since fingerprints are derived from pooled intermediate activations (64-dimensional vectors after global average pooling) rather than raw input pixels, reconstructing training images from fingerprints alone would additionally require inverting the teacher's convolutional backbone, which is a substantially harder problem. Stronger privacy guarantees would require additional mechanisms such as using $r < C$, quantization, noise injection, or formal differential privacy, which we leave to future work.

\subsection{Limitations}

CSD has several limitations:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy gap.} CSD trails KD by approximately 0.92 percentage points on CIFAR-100. The teacher's output probability distribution contains information that fingerprints at this architecture scale do not fully capture.
    \item \textbf{Per-sample storage.} The per-sample variant requires more storage than the teacher (24.41\,MB vs.\ 3.29\,MB). Only the per-class variant achieves net storage reduction. Per-sample fingerprints are best used in privacy-sensitive settings where the teacher cannot be shared.
    \item \textbf{Single architecture family and dataset.} We evaluate only on ResNet variants for CIFAR-100. Generalization to larger-scale datasets, Vision Transformers, or large language models is not yet demonstrated.
    \item \textbf{Single seed.} All accuracy results use a single random seed (42). On CIFAR-100, ResNet-20 accuracy can vary by $\pm$0.3--0.5\,pp across seeds, which is comparable to the +0.88\,pp gain of CSD per-class. Therefore the reported improvements should be interpreted as preliminary findings rather than statistically confirmed effects. A multi-seed study with mean and standard deviation is required for stronger conclusions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Work}

Several extensions of CSD are worth exploring:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hybrid with KD:} CSD fingerprints and KD operate on complementary teacher knowledge (feature geometry vs.\ output probabilities). Since fingerprint precomputation is a one-time cost, combining CSD with KD would impose no additional teacher overhead beyond what KD already requires, and could recover a larger fraction of KD's accuracy gain.
    \item \textbf{Generality:} Extending CSD to Vision Transformers (CLS token fingerprints) and large language models (hidden-state fingerprints) would demonstrate that random-projection fingerprints are architecture-agnostic.
    \item \textbf{Fingerprint quantization:} Quantizing each fingerprint entry from float32 to int8 would reduce storage by an additional 4$\times$ (from 50\,KB to 12.5\,KB), with minimal accuracy degradation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced Cosine Similarity Distillation (CSD), a distillation method that removes the teacher from the student training loop by shifting all teacher computation to a one-time offline fingerprint precomputation phase. CSD precomputes compact fingerprints---cosine similarities of the teacher's intermediate features against a frozen random matrix---and trains the student to regress these fingerprints alongside the cross-entropy loss. The teacher is never loaded during student training.

On CIFAR-100 with ResNet-20/56, in a single-seed experiment, CSD recovers 48.9\% of KD's accuracy gain (CSD per-class: 69.65\% vs. baseline 68.77\%) while reducing teacher-side storage by 67$\times$ (from 3.29\,MB to 50\,KB) and removing the teacher from GPU memory during student training. Ablation studies validate the core mechanism, showing that student-teacher fingerprint alignment steadily increases to over 0.85 during training. Iterative refinement across five versions yielded our final design.

CSD explores a direction for deployment-efficient distillation: its simplicity, storage efficiency, and teacher-model hiding property make it attractive for settings where the teacher cannot be shared or where GPU memory is constrained during student training. Future work with multiple seeds, larger datasets, and formal privacy mechanisms will further strengthen these findings.
